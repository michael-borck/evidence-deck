A DECK OF DEFENSIBLE JUDGEMENTS

The Evidence Deck

23 cards of evidence discipline for anyone evaluating AI: whether it works, for whom, and under which conditions.
Anyone can demo AI. The edge is proving it works.
CLAIMS01 / 23

Policy versus control

WHY IT GOES WRONG

A written policy reads like an accomplished fact, and the audit inherits the feeling instead of the truth.

THE MOVE

A policy is a claim about how the organisation intends to operate. A control is the practice that actually reduces risk. Audit the practice.

A policy is a claim. A control is a claim that has survived testing.

CLAIMS02 / 23

The board does not need a policy

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Boards are shown documents and asked to feel reassured.

THE MOVE

The board needs to know whether the policy is true. A report that repeats management's assurances is not assurance; a report that tests them is.

The board does not need a policy. It needs to know whether the policy is true.

CLAIMS03 / 23

The demo versus the deployment

WHY IT GOES WRONG

A working demo proves the solution runs. Runs is not reliable, and the gap between them is where applied AI lives.

THE MOVE

Read every demo as an assertion: a claim that has not yet met its evidence. A deployment has to survive inputs the demo never showed you.

A demo is engineered to be impressive. A deployment has to work.

CLAIMS04 / 23

Fluency is camouflage

WHY IT GOES WRONG

The smooth answer gets a pass because it reads well.

THE MOVE

Fluency is not a signal of correctness or fairness; it is camouflage. Separate how it reads from whether it holds.

The most dangerous AI output is the one nobody checked.

CLAIMS05 / 23

Agreement is not evidence

WHY IT GOES WRONG

A session where the AI was right every time feels like proof of reliability.

THE MOVE

A demo persuades; a log proves — because the log shows the work, including where the AI was confidently wrong and you caught it. If the log reads as a smooth collaboration, you have written persuasion, not proof.

Agreement is not evidence.

GATES06 / 23

Gate one: prototype to bounded pilot

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Polished prototypes get shown to clients as if they were systems.

THE MOVE

Before a bounded pilot: defined use, held-out evaluation against the brief, data and security review, named owner, tested fallback, limits on users, actions, duration and volume. Missing any of it? Keep it in development.

A polished prototype is not a production system.

GATES07 / 23

Gate two: pilot to production

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Pilot scope quietly widens, and the pilot becomes production by drift.

THE MOVE

Before production: results from representative conditions, measured review workload, incident and rollback rehearsal, monitoring and access controls, approvals from the responsible teams. Missing any? Extend, redesign or stop — do not widen the scope.

Do not quietly widen the scope.

GATES08 / 23

Gate three: continued operation

WHY IT GOES WRONG

The go-live is treated as the finish line, and the evaluation never runs again.

THE MOVE

Continued operation requires scheduled and change-triggered evaluation, monitoring of relevant failures, current ownership, and action on threshold breaches. The question changes from "does it work?" to "is it still working, and how would I know if it stopped?"

Is it still working — and how would you know if it stopped?

GATES09 / 23

Re-baselining

WHY IT GOES WRONG

A disappointing result gets rescued by rewriting the acceptance rule.

THE MOVE

After any change to model, prompt, source or workflow: keep the old evidence and run a new comparison. A changed threshold is a new decision to explain, not a way to rescue a score.

A changed threshold is a new decision to explain.

GATES10 / 23

The dangerous practitioner

WHY IT GOES WRONG

The line between prototype and production is invisible from the inside.

THE MOVE

The dangerous practitioner is the one who ships a polished prototype as if it were a production system. Say where the line is before you cross it.

Name the line before you ship.

MEASUREMENT11 / 23

Headline accuracy is an assertion

WHY IT GOES WRONG

"90% accurate" lands as evidence, and nobody asks the rest of the sentence.

THE MOVE

90% on which class, at what cost to the others? Never report an overall number without the per-class and per-group breakdown beneath it.

Headline accuracy is an assertion, not evidence.

MEASUREMENT12 / 23

A model without a threshold

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Evaluation without an acceptance rule can't fail, which means it can't pass either.

THE MOVE

State the threshold in the sentence: for this decision, good enough means this number, because this is the blast radius. And a changed threshold is a new decision to explain.

A model without a stated threshold is a demo, not an evaluation.

MEASUREMENT13 / 23

The worst-group gap

WHY IT GOES WRONG

The average hides the group the system fails.

THE MOVE

Compute accuracy per condition and per group. The worst-group gap — the spread between best and worst condition — is the honest headline.

The worst-group gap is the honest headline.

MEASUREMENT14 / 23

Adversarial testing

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Most testing asks "does it work on normal inputs?" — the conditions that actually bite are never tried.

THE MOVE

Ask the opposite: what inputs make it fail? A customer photographs the router in a dark cupboard at midnight, because that is when the fault showed up. A demo evaluated on the happy path has proven nothing about those conditions.

Break it on purpose, before someone else does it for you.

MEASUREMENT15 / 23

Small samples

WHY IT GOES WRONG

A perfect result on a handful of examples reads as dependability.

THE MOVE

A perfect score on three examples does not establish dependable performance, and a single successful test supports "it worked once", never "it is tested regularly".

A sample that cannot support the claim is an anecdote with a date.

RATINGS16 / 23

The defensible Partial

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Boards prefer clean ratings, and the auditor supplies the clean rating the evidence cannot carry.

THE MOVE

A defensible Partial says: this part works, this part does not, this part is unknown. It gives the board a decision — accept the gap, fund the fix, or investigate. A Met with no evidence gives comfort that has not been earned.

A defensible Partial is worth more than an unsupported Met.

RATINGS17 / 23

Triangulation

WHY IT GOES WRONG

One source — a policy, an interview, a log — gets treated as the whole answer.

THE MOVE

Three evidence moves: inspect what is said, inquire what people believe, observe what the system recorded. Together they support a rating; alone, each is a claim, a belief or a partial record. If sources disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

If they disagree, the disagreement is the finding.

RATINGS18 / 23

The limitation beside the rating

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Limitations get filed in an appendix nobody reads, far from the rating they qualify.

THE MOVE

Record the limitation beside the rating, plus one sentence on what would change it: "MFA moves to Met if the legacy exception is closed and the log gap is explained."

Limitations belong beside the rating.

RATINGS19 / 23

Refusing to rate

WHY IT GOES WRONG

Thin evidence still pressures a rating, because a blank looks like failure.

THE MOVE

A good answer refuses to rate when the evidence is thin — and that refusal is itself a finding. Confidence is a delivery property, not a truth property.

A refusal to rate is a finding.

RECORDS20 / 23

The proof brief, before building

WHY IT GOES WRONG

The reasoning gets reconstructed after the results are known, which is persuasion with extra steps.

THE MOVE

Write the brief before you build: intended use, affected people, baseline, acceptance rule with the blast radius, evaluation plan, data boundary, stop rule, evidence record.

Start the brief now. Don't reconstruct the reasoning after the results.

RECORDS21 / 23

The dossier

WHY IT GOES WRONG

The evidence lives in twelve places and dies in none of them, so nothing can be defended.

THE MOVE

One cumulative dossier: brief · sources and boundaries · design · evaluation · review · decision and handover. The product of applied AI is not a model; it is a defensible judgement, and the dossier is where the defence lives.

The product is a defensible judgement.

RECORDS22 / 23

The conversation log

WHY IT GOES WRONG

AI sessions get remembered as collaborations in which the assistant was right.

THE MOVE

Log per session: date, models, goal, the prompts that mattered, where the AI was wrong and how you corrected it, what you decided to trust and why — plus the unedited transcript. The unedited record is the integrity gate.

A demo persuades; a log proves.

RECORDS23 / 23

The threshold sentence

WHY IT GOES WRONG

"Good enough" floats free of the decision, the number and the blast radius.

THE MOVE

One sentence per evaluation: "For [decision], good enough means [threshold], because [blast radius]." Then an accuracy figure measured once is a photograph — schedule the re-checks.

For this decision, good enough means this, because of this.
